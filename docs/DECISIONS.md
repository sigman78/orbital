## Proposed engineering choices

The prior plan proposed a conventional Vulkan compatibility path within a NoGraphicsAPI fork, artistic scene scale, a staged runnable implementation, and quality scaling. Treat these as the working direction, subject to the foundation spike. Do not assume compatibility has been established or that newer hardware automatically supports upstream extensions.

HDR means floating-point scene rendering and tone-mapped SDR output for the first release. Native HDR display presentation is deferred. Performance figures in the PRD are targets, not measured guarantees.

## 2026-09-11 — implementation resolutions

- The conventional NoGraphicsAPI fallback is implemented and demonstrated on GTX 1080 Ti; details and upstream pin are in FOUNDATION.md. Demo shaders use this ABI even on newer hardware. This is not a replacement graphics library.
- Default shaders use Slang, offline SPIR-V compilation and scene_shared.h shared data layouts. Compiler binaries are pinned by bootstrap.ps1.
- The material pipeline loads realistic sourced maps with provenance; the earlier procedural texture generator was removed. Textures are stored as PNG and decoded with the vendored Wuffs decoder, so loading has no platform dependency. See ASSETS.md.
- Rendering is linear floating-point HDR internally, with ACES-fit tone mapping and sRGB presentation to an SDR swapchain. Native HDR-monitor output remains deferred.
- Longitude-wrap derivatives are corrected for spherical texture sampling. Temporal reprojection and area-weighted tiny-asteroid billboards address aliasing; residual phase/motion artifacts require the evidence in STABILITY.md, not a blanket resolved claim.
- Scene distances are compressed for an authored fictional-system tour. Giant/Earth radii are substantially differentiated; this is not a true-scale Solar System simulation.
- RTX 4080 quality/performance sign-off remains open. Only GTX 1080 Ti has been exercised locally.
- Per subsequent user direction, belt budgets increased 10x to 35k/65k. Seeded plane-cut polyhedra replace smooth displaced rocks, with split face normals and scanned dielectric PBR roughness. A separate polar cluster index conservatively rejects whole offscreen/planet-occluded regions while preserving original IDs and nested tiers. Existing four mesh-instance batches plus one distant-billboard batch avoid per-object draw calls. This is CPU clustered culling, not GPU indirect compaction or hierarchical-Z occlusion.
- Render prettify pass: the atmosphere march is clipped by the opaque depth buffer so rocks in front of a shell are never blended over. Rock shadows on the giant are replaced by an analytic belt shadow (ring profile shared between geometry::belt_ring_density and the shader). Surface detail is procedural where the source maps run out (scaled by texel magnification): Earth land grain and cloud structure, cloud shadows on the ground, Jupiter zonal drift with domain-warped swirls and layered turbulence, Moon relief and self-shadowing derived from the albedo (broad brightness as terrain, small bright features sunk as craters; no height map is sourced). The belt is thinned into rings, its radial bands orbit at slightly different rates, and every rock tumbles at its own rate. Lens ghosts are soft oblique ellipses along the sun axis instead of rings. The gas map is stored at 2K to hide JPEG blocking.
- Mars and its moons, sourced relief: the body list is data driven (up to eight; the renderer finds the terrestrial, gas giant and desert anchors by class). Mars is at true size relative to Earth with a thin dusty atmosphere; Phobos and Deimos are enlarged and orbit fast enough to watch, drawn as seeded rocks with the scanned rock PBR maps. The Moon and Mars shade from NASA elevation models baked into normal+height maps (LOLA, MOLA) instead of relief guessed from albedo; short height-field traces give crater-wall self-shadowing. Exposure adaptation is centre weighted and asymmetric in real time (fast to close down, slow to open), within about a stop; the final composite adds faint lateral chromatic aberration and midtone film grain.
- Rock library and material: rocks are icospheres displaced by a seeded shape function (lumps, ridged noise, bowl craters with rims, plane cuts) generated at six subdivision levels from one function, so normals and silhouettes agree across levels; 16 shapes times 6 levels are drawn as instanced groups by projected size. Meshes are indexed with shared vertices and shape-function normals, ready for meshoptimizer meshlets and mesh shaders later; tessellation was rejected because the GPU layer has no such stages and parallax covers the close-range relief. Three 2K CC0 rock sets are triplanar mapped in object space with a seeded primary/secondary blend and parallax occlusion on the dominant plane of close rocks.
- GPU-driven belt culling replaces the CPU cluster pass: a compute shader runs one thread per rock (frustum with jitter padding, analytic occlusion by every body, projected-size level pick, billboard demotion), then a count, prefix and scatter sequence writes per-group instance lists and indirect draw arguments that the CPU submits as one indirect draw per group. Everything goes through buffer addresses because the conventional descriptor set has no storage buffers; the fork gained conventional-backend paths for compute pipelines and indirect draws. Depth-pyramid occlusion was not added: the only occluders that matter here are the bodies, which the analytic test covers, and the conventional path has no storage images to build a pyramid with. Measured at the frozen belt view, 1080p, GTX 1080 Ti (`--rocks` overrides the belt size for such runs): at 65k rocks the CPU pass took 1.25 ms per frame and the GPU pass 0.21 ms of CPU; at 650k rocks the CPU pass took 13.5 ms and the frame 18.9 ms, the GPU pass 0.23 ms of CPU and a 6.9 ms frame. The benchmark CSV records the CPU preparation time and the GPU pass timings per frame separately from the vsync-bound frame time; the culling dispatches plus the shadow pass measure 0.09 ms at 65k rocks and 0.25 ms at 520k, so the belt's GPU cost is its rendering, not its culling.
- Belt budgets rose 8x to 280k baseline and 520k high quality once culling moved to the GPU. The rock library is uploaded as one pooled vertex and index range and drawn with a single indexed multi-draw whose per-group slices (first index, vertex offset, first instance) the prefix pass writes; the prefix pass also compacts empty groups out and writes the draw count for `draw_indexed_indirect_count`. Measured at the frozen belt view, 1080p: pooling took the GPU median from 5.13 to 5.06 ms at 65k and from 5.86 to 5.79 ms at 520k and removed the 520k frame-time spikes (p95 10.9 to 7.1 ms) that recording 97 draws caused; count compaction changed nothing measurable, since the GPU already skips zero-instance draws, and is kept because it is free and bounds the multi-draw to live groups. The title bar shows frame time, a rolling p95, GPU time, draw calls, live rock groups, visible rocks and triangles.
- Belt self-shadowing is analytic extinction, not a shadow map. A belt-wide orthographic depth map (4096 texels over the annulus in light space, casters of radius 0.05 and above culled by a second compute pass, 0.41 ms at 520k rocks) was built and rejected: the belt is 1.4 units thick and mostly empty, so rock-on-rock occlusion was rare and only visible on close rocks, and the sun's one-degree disc makes real shadows at these distances mostly penumbra, so the hard 0.03-unit texels shimmered as the casters tumbled. Instead each rock pixel integrates the belt's density (ring profile times a vertical falloff) along its sun ray and attenuates by exp(-tau); rocks on the sunward rim stay bright, buried and far-side rocks sit in a smooth dusk, billboards included. It is stable by construction, consistent with the belt's shadow on the giant, and costs about 0.17 ms in the surface pass. The belt's shadow on the gas giant was strengthened 30 percent at the same time. Screen-space contact shadows were tried and reverted: crisp and stable on rocks directly behind a neighbour, but few rocks qualify in a belt this sparse, for about 0.4 ms. A belt transmittance map sits on top instead (F3 toggles it, F4 the extinction, so the two can be compared live): the size-tail rocks (radius 0.03 and above, about 5% of the belt, kept as a compact second record array so only they run through the splat) add their cross-section additively into a 2048-texel light-space map over the whole belt, into one of four depth slices measured along the sun ray from the texel column's crossing of the belt plane, and a separable blur widens it into the sun's penumbra. A receiver sums the slices on its sun side (never its own, which holds its own splat) and attenuates by exp(-coverage). It is stable by construction and shades rocks behind neighbours softly, but it too is sparse in effect, and it costs about 0.6 ms at 520k rocks (0.4 ms of splat, clear and blur, 0.4 ms of lookup, partly overlapping). Splatting every rock cost 1.4 ms: the vertex work over the whole belt dominated, a 1024 map saved only 0.2 ms and a radius test inside the vertex shader nothing, hence the compact caster list. Small rocks cover under a fifth of a texel each and made no visible difference.
- Belt population shaped after the main belt: the radial profile keeps its three rings and gap but gains a sharp resonance-style inner edge (4% of the width) and an exponential outer tail (e-folding 14% of the width, reaching 1.5 widths) so the belt fades to nothing; heights are Gaussian with a scale height that flares from 0.6 to 1.4 half thicknesses across the width, as inclinations grow outward; sizes follow a power law between 0.5 and 12 reference scales with differential exponent 3 (Dohnanyi's 3.5 made the median rock a sub-pixel speck and the belt vanished at distance); composition follows the taxonomic gradient, bright reddish silicate rocks dominating inside and dark neutral carbonaceous rocks outside, with 3% grey metallic ones, the class chosen per rock by radius and chance, carried in the instance tint's integer part, and also picking which scanned rock set the material blends. The old fixed size tail (4x and 9x by id) is gone; the belt shadow, extinction and transmittance box all follow the same tapered profile.
- Anti-aliasing rework against shimmer on small and peripheral rocks, measured as the pixel difference between two consecutive jittered frames of a frozen scene at 1080p. Three causes: the temporal pass rejected history whenever stored and reprojected depth disagreed, so a pixel flipping between a thin rock and the sky never converged; history blended in linear HDR, so bright specks dominated and flickered; and meshes under two pixels alias by construction. The temporal pass now only rejects on a depth mismatch when both frames saw a surface, clips history to the 3x3 neighbourhood in YCoCg by variance (1.5 sigma), blends with luminance weights, and fetches history with a Catmull-Rom filter; rocks under 2.5 pixels of radius become the analytic disc billboards; and an FXAA pass (edge-end search plus sub-pixel blend) runs over the tone-mapped image through an intermediate so captures include it. F5 cycles off, temporal, temporal plus FXAA (the default), and --aa sets it for scripted runs. Pixels changing by more than 48 levels between frames: distant belt view 917 to 28, dense belt view 4952 to 2110; pixels changing by more than 16: 7894 to 3270 and 17351 to 17285 (the residual is sub-pixel texture motion on close rocks). Cost about 0.6 ms at 520k rocks, mostly the larger billboards, of which FXAA is 0.07 ms.
- Sub-pixel rocks are lit once per rock in the culling pass and drawn as pixel-filter footprints, not shaded discs. The disc billboards spread a rock's brightness over a disc the size of the cut-off, so a 0.3-pixel rock read as a dim 5-pixel blob, and every blob pixel ran the full lighting, extinction and transmittance lookup. Now the cull pass computes the radiance of each splat once: a Lambertian sphere's disc-averaged brightness at its sun-rock-camera phase angle (the analytic phase function, 2/3 at full phase, no bake), times the body eclipse, belt extinction and transmittance map, times the class colour and the average scanned albedo; the fragment only shapes a Gaussian footprint no narrower than 0.8 pixels whose alpha integrates to the rock's true covered area. Splats stay small and dim at distance, the belt resolves as fine grain, and the surface pass drops from 3.50 to 3.01 ms at 520k rocks for 0.13 ms more in the cull pass. Spherical harmonics per shape would add the rock's own view dependence on top and remain an option; the shared belt lighting moved into shaders/belt.slang, which both the surface shaders and the cull pass include. Calibrated afterwards against mesh-only renders (the --splat option scripts the cut-off): the first point splats saturated a full pixel for rocks near one pixel wide, which a mesh only covers some frames, so the far field read brighter as more rocks became splats even though the energy matched within a few percent. Splats now have two regimes, a soft-edged disc of the rock's true size above half a pixel and a one-pixel Gaussian point below it whose peak alpha stays under one; the disc is scaled to the shapes' projected area (0.8 of the bounding disc), the phase function gains a small forward-scatter rim (0.12) standing in for the meshes' grazing specular, and each splat uses the coarsest mip of the scanned set its class leads with. Luminance over the pixels that change with the cut-off, relative to meshes only: far view 0.97 / 0.99 / 0.99 and belt view 0.98 / 1.02 / 1.07 at the 1.2, 2.5 and 4 pixel cut-offs. Disc splats are then shaded as sphere impostors: the cull pass stores albedo times irradiance plus the per-rock rim and ambient fractions, and the fragment takes the Lambert cosine per pixel from the sphere normal the disc coordinate implies, so a few-pixel rock shows a lit side and a dark limb like its mesh. The disc average of that cosine is the phase function the points use, so the calibration holds (0.99 and 0.98 at the 2.5 and 4 pixel cut-offs in the belt view) at no measurable cost. The count pass no longer lights splats at all, since it only needs each rock's group; the scatter pass does it once. F7 switches the lighting back on in both passes for comparison: 0.82 versus 0.75 ms of cull time at 520k rocks, identical images.
- Three tone curves selectable at runtime (F8, --tone): ACES filmic as Stephen Hill's fit of the RRT and sRGB ODT (replacing the Narkowicz approximation), AgX in its minimal form (inset matrix, log2 encode over the working range, sixth-order sigmoid, outset, decode) and Khronos PBR Neutral (hue preserving, linear to 0.8 then a shoulder with slight desaturation). All matrix products are written as row dot products so the column-major layout flag for buffer matrices cannot change their meaning. ACES stays the default; AgX lifts shadows and flattens highlights, Neutral is the most literal and the most saturated.
- Per-curve exposure trims, applied to the frame exposure before the curve: AgX 0.37, PBR Neutral 0.75, ACES 1. Fitted against captures rather than a grey ramp, because the curves' toes behave differently on coloured shadows (Neutral's black offset uses the minimum channel, ACES desaturates): the geometric mean of the per-pixel display luminance ratio to ACES over pixels ACES shows at 0.005 to 0.03 was 1.35 for AgX and 1.5 to 1.75 for Neutral untrimmed, and is 1.15 for both (belt view 1.17 and 1.41) with the trims; mids sit at 0.7 (AgX) and 0.88 (Neutral) of ACES, the price of AgX's flat shoulder and of matching the shadows. Untrimmed, the curves compared brightness more than rendering intent.
- Base exposure gain of 1.5 ahead of the tone curves. The Hill ACES fit sits about a stop under the Narkowicz approximation it replaced (scene 0.18 maps to 0.11 display linear against 0.27), so the switch made every view darker; the gain brings the scene back toward the earlier brightness while keeping the user's exposure at 1 and the +/- keys meaningful. The per-curve trims stay, so the curves still match in the shadows.
- Khronos PBR Neutral is the default tone curve (--tone 2), chosen by eye after the base gain: it is the most literal of the three and keeps the belt colours and Jupiter bands saturated. ACES filmic and AgX remain on F8.
- Rock splats are drawn into their own layer (premultiplied rgba16f, slot 31, depth tested against the scene but after the atmosphere) without the jitter, and the post pass composites the layer over the temporally anti-aliased image. Splats faded to nothing while the camera translated: a sub-pixel feature that moves a fraction of a pixel per frame is resampled and clipped out of the history every frame, whatever the reprojection does (giving the splats depth for exact reprojection recovered only part of it, and a lower history weight under motion nothing). Measured on a fixed-rate lateral pan (--pan, a development option that adds a constant to the strafe axis and steps the camera at 60 Hz so runs compare): the dim specks of the no-AA image kept a median 0.30 to 0.35 of their peak and 0.62 to 0.67 of their 5x5 energy under the old pass, 1.00 and 0.99 with the layer; static frames are unchanged. The splats are smooth by construction (Gaussian points, soft-edged discs lit once per rock), so they need no temporal filter; only the depth test against the jittered scene remains, which costs nothing measurable in frame-to-frame flicker (2976 against 2990 pixels changing by more than 48 levels in the dense belt view, mean change 0.87 against 0.93). GPU frame 6.56 ms against 6.77 before at 520k rocks.
- FXAA no longer smears distant rocks: the sub-pixel blend strength drops from 0.75 to 0.5, a dim pixel brighter than all four neighbours (a far rock, not an edge; highlights on close rocks stay above the 0.35 luma cap and keep the filter) passes through, and so does any edge whose end search stops within 2.5 texels, which is a few-pixel rock. Specks in the far belt keep 0.96 of their no-AA luminance against 0.85 before, the same as the temporal pass alone. The price is that the residual flicker of 3 to 4 pixel mesh rocks is no longer blurred away: pixels changing by more than 48 levels between frames rise from 2976 to 4190 in the dense belt view, while the count above 16 levels and the mean change stay level. Raising the splat cut-off (F6) turns those rocks into stable splats. SMAA would be the next step if the trade still shows.
- The splat layer is gone again: taking the splats out of the temporal pass made every sub-pixel rock twinkle as it crossed pixel centres (a one-pixel Gaussian's brightest pixel changes 4x with sub-pixel position, and widening it to 0.6 px sigma still leaves 2x), which reads as embers. The splats are back in the HDR image, jittered and temporally filtered like everything else, and the fade is fixed inside the pass instead: after the atmosphere, the splat quads write their depth and a mask (slot 31, r8) over their whole footprint, so the temporal pass reprojects them exactly as rocks at their distance, keeps their history unclipped (the neighbourhood clip is what destroyed a moving sub-pixel feature; with exact reprojection the history over splat pixels holds 0.95 of the current frame's energy, so the clip has nothing to fix), and shortens their history under motion (0.95 at rest to 0.3 at 4 px per frame; a splat is already a filtered footprint, so a short history keeps it sharp where a long one smeared it over five resampled frames). On the fixed-rate pan the dim specks now keep a median 0.72 / 0.68 of their peak and 0.86 / 0.85 of their 5x5 energy at 3 and 6 units of strafe per second (0.30 / 0.35 and 0.62 / 0.67 before any of this; 1.00 with the layer, which twinkled), 1.00 on a slow pan and at rest; static flicker is level with the FXAA-tuned build (3902 pixels changing by more than 48 levels in the dense belt view).
- SMAA 1x as the spatial anti-aliasing pass, default on top of the temporal pass, with FXAA kept as an option. The toggles split: F5 (--taa) switches the temporal pass, F9 (--spatial) cycles the spatial pass through off, FXAA and SMAA. SMAA is the reference algorithm (preset HIGH: threshold 0.1, 16 search steps, 8 diagonal steps, 25% corner rounding) ported to Slang as three fullscreen passes over the tone-mapped image, with the reference area and search textures shipped as byte arrays widened to RGBA8; the vertex-side offsets are computed per pixel and the texel-offset fetches shift the coordinate instead, since SPIR-V wants constant offsets. Luma is taken in gamma space, as the edge detection expects, from the sRGB intermediate. Against the tuned FXAA on the dense belt view with the temporal pass on: pixels changing by more than 48 levels between frames 3120 against 3900 (2990 with the original FXAA, which blurred the specks), far specks keep 0.93 of their no-AA luminance against 0.96, and the post pass costs 0.80 against 0.68 ms at 1080p (whole GPU frame 7.00 against 6.83 ms). The texture descriptor count of the conventional backend rises from 32 to 40 for the two lookup tables and the two SMAA intermediates.
- Pixel-sized post effects are tuned in a reference frame (the 1600x900 default window) and keep their pixel size in any other window: the chromatic fringe takes its radius from the reference aspect and converts its shift through the reference resolution, so a 3440x1440 borderless window no longer shows stars split into separate red, green and blue dots at the corners (the old uv-space cubic grew with both the resolution and the ultrawide corner radius, 15 pixels of fringe against 3 in the default window); the bloom tap spacing scales with the frame height instead, so the halo keeps its size relative to the picture. Angular effects (vignette, lens flare) already work in normalised coordinates; grain stays per pixel.
- Dear ImGui (1.91.9b, vendored under third_party/imgui) provides a control panel docked to the right edge, F12 to show it and --ui to start with it: frame statistics with a frame-time plot and the GPU pass breakdown, and every toggle the hotkeys reach, grouped as quality, anti-aliasing, belt, tone, camera and overlay; the capture key moves to F10. Layering: the ImGui core is platform-agnostic and lives with the application (src/app/ui.cpp builds the panel from AppState, now in app/app_state.hpp); the Win32 backend is compiled into the platform layer behind platform/overlay.hpp, which attaches it through a platform-internal message hook on the window (platform/win32/window_access.hpp) that also consumes clicks, wheel and typed keys while the panel wants them, so no OS type reaches the application; the draw lists are rendered by the renderer's own backend (shaders/ui.slang) into the swapchain pass after the present triangle, from a 4 MiB overlay region at the end of the dynamic heap, with ImDrawVert repacked into 32-byte records so the pointer layout is unambiguous and the font atlas in texture slot 36. Captures do not include the panel, since they read the frame before presentation.
- Belt dust as a scattering medium (F11, --dust): a view-ray march through the same analytic belt density the rock extinction uses (rings, gap, flared height), so the fine matter between the rocks glows where they are dense and thins at the edges; each sample takes sunlight through the belt, the planets' shadows and the rock transmittance map, and scatters it with a forward-peaked Henyey-Greenstein phase (g 0.55) mixed with an isotropic share (0.35) for multiple scattering and back-scatter; what lies behind is attenuated by the view transmittance (premultiplied blend after the atmospheres). The dust's own extinction is 0.08 of the rock coefficient: the rock extinction caps its sun path at one belt width, the view ray does not, and at the full coefficient an in-plane chord was opaque grey. Seen from outside the belt the column through the thin medium vanishes, so the density rises with the camera's distance from the slab (times 30 at six slab half-heights out), which turns the belt into a faint disc with the giant's shadow cut through it, the distant counterpart the user asked for, without a second code path. The march runs at half resolution (16 view samples, 2 sun samples, jittered per pixel and frame) into its own target and is composited with a depth-aware four-tap upsample, so the haze does not bleed across rock silhouettes; the temporal pass settles the jitter (frame-to-frame flicker is lower with the dust than without, since it fills the black between rocks). Cost at 1080p, 520k rocks: 0.8 ms in the belt, 0.3 ms in the far view (the full-resolution first cut was 3.9 ms). Brightness: the belt view gains 11% mean luminance, the far belt band 1.7 to 2x.

## Linux desktop port (2026-09-12)

Implemented in `5a757ae` on `linux-port`. The starting point built only the CPU layers on Linux;
CMake rejected the desktop demo, and NoGraphicsAPI rejected non-Windows presentation. The complete
demo now builds and renders on the Linux PC. Setup and repeatable commands are in [LINUX.md](LINUX.md).

### Platform and build

- Added `src/platform/linux/` implementations of all four platform interfaces. SDL2 handles Wayland
  or X11 windows, event polling, relative mouse look, key presses, focus, minimize, maximize and
  borderless fullscreen. The native handle passed through the renderer is an `SDL_Window*` on Linux.
- Vendored the SDL2 Dear ImGui backend from the same 1.91.9b tag as the existing core. SDL events feed
  ImGui before application input; mouse and keyboard capture prevent panel input from also driving
  the camera. Fontconfig selects regular/bold sans-serif fonts and FreeType rasterizes UTF-8 HUD text
  into the existing RGBA overlay format. Linux locates assets relative to `/proc/self/exe`.
- CMake selects the platform implementation and its dependencies. Added `linux-release`,
  `linux-debug` and `linux-cpu` presets, and `tools/bootstrap.sh`, which verifies the SHA-256 of the
  pinned Slang 2026.14.1 Linux archive before installing it under ignored `.tools/slang`.
  `spirv-val` validates generated shaders when installed. System Vulkan, SDL2, FreeType and Fontconfig
  development packages are documented separately from shader bootstrap.
- The NoGraphicsAPI package configuration also resolves SDL2 on Linux. Regenerated
  `third_party/NoGraphicsAPI-compat.patch` against upstream `8e414bd0a8010b9f721d06d470860e27aa69c071`
  and checked that applying it reproduces all four modified upstream files.

### Vulkan presentation and device compatibility

| Problem found on the Linux PC | Implemented change | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Presentation was guarded by `_WIN32`. | Obtain instance extensions from SDL, create the SDL Vulkan surface, and enable swapchain/maintenance extensions on Linux. | The existing renderer presents through native Wayland or XWayland. |
| Wayland reports `UINT_MAX` for the application-selected surface extent. | Query SDL's drawable size, clamp it to surface limits, and use that result for swapchain creation, resize detection and drawable-size queries. | Startup, resize and fullscreen work with compositor-selected pixel dimensions. A requested 960x540 window measured 961x540 on this desktop. |
| Mesa exposed 81 surface formats, exceeding the backend's fixed limit of 64. | Raise the format enumeration capacity to 256. | Device initialization reaches swapchain creation on this driver. |
| Device selection required 64-bit timestamps; this Intel queue reports 36 valid bits. | Accept queues with nonzero timestamp precision and expose `DeviceCaps::timestamp_valid_bits`. | The GPU is accepted and the renderer can account for counter wrap. |
| Intel lacks `shaderStorageImageReadWithoutFormat`, which Orbital's shaders do not use. | Make it optional for the conventional backend; enable it only when supported and expose `storage_image_read_without_format` in the device capabilities. | Device creation succeeds without claiming unsupported shader functionality. The experimental backend retains its requirement. |

### Renderer and shader corrections

- GPU pass timings now use `core/timing.hpp::timestamp_ticks` to mask timestamp differences to the
  device's valid counter width before converting ticks to milliseconds. This covers total GPU time
  and shadow, surface, atmosphere and post timings. Compile-time tests cover ordinary intervals,
  36-bit wrap, 64-bit wrap and a zero-width counter.
- Khronos validation reported calls to `vkCmdDrawIndexedIndirectCount` without the Vulkan 1.2
  `drawIndirectCount` feature enabled. Device selection now checks it, and device creation enables
  it. This is the existing compacted asteroid multi-draw path.
- Validation also reported Slang-generated `DemoteToHelperInvocation` capabilities without the
  matching Vulkan 1.3 feature. Device selection and creation now check and enable
  `shaderDemoteToHelperInvocation` for those fragment shaders.
- The shader texture arrays had 40 entries while the CPU's `Slot::count` was 38. Validation reported
  uninitialized descriptors starting at slot 38. `ORBITAL_TEXTURE_COUNT` in `scene_shared.h` now
  supplies the array size to `common.slang`, `cull.slang`, `ui.slang` and the CPU slot count. The
  existing fallback-binding loop consequently initializes reserved slots 38 and 39 as well.
- Explicitly initialized the pixel containers in two renderer image constructions to remove GCC
  missing-field warnings. The scene's lighting, atmospheric scattering, asteroid population,
  material model, tone mapping and anti-aliasing algorithms retain their existing behavior; the
  rendering changes above concern valid resource bindings, enabled features and timing accuracy.

- The belt has two representations blended by the camera's distance: full detail inside and near it (the rock meshes and splats, the half-resolution dust march), and a baked disc once the camera is out (fade from 0.5 to 2 belt widths past the slab, smoothstep, scaled by the panel's far-belt fade distance). The disc is two maps over the belt plane in an orthographic frame: the sunlight reaching the plane through the belt and the planets' shadows (1024 texels, baked every 16 frames while the far tier is in use), and the rocks' coverage times albedo, splatted from the rock records every 32 frames (256 texels so each texel averages many rocks; rocks above 0.06 world radius stay out of it and keep their splats and meshes, since they are rare and dominated their texel as bright blotches). The far tier is not a plane lookup, which breaks down edge-on, but a 12-sample march through the slab's thickness: the analytic density for the dust, the vertical profile applied to the baked rock coverage, the baked sunlight for both, sampled with at least a 3x3 box and wider with minification. The near paths carry the complementary weight (splat coverage and the dust march scale by one minus the weight), so the crossfade never pops. The distance-scaled density hack of the earlier dust pass is gone; in its place an explicit legibility boost ramps in with the weight (25x for the dust, 8x for the rock map, times the far-belt brightness slider), because at true scale the far belt is faint: the dust is thin and the rocks cover a fraction of a percent of the plane. Measured at 1080p, 520k rocks: far Jupiter view 5.55 ms against 5.32 with the old hack, the Earth view unchanged at 5.38, inside the belt unchanged.

- Earth sea state and cloud shadows are panel tweakables (an Earth section) carried in two frame float4s. - Cloud shadows now fall on the air as well as the ground: the atmosphere pass shades the air below the cloud layer by the same coverage, so over the sea, where Rayleigh haze rather than the dark water carries most of the light, a cloud's shadow reads as a darker patch of haze the way it does from orbit; before, the unshadowed haze filled the shadow back in and cloud shadows seemed to apply to land only. - The gas giant's cloud deck is animated by flow-map advection instead of a noise warp. tools/bake-flow-map.py bakes gas_flow.png (1024x512, east and south flow in texture units per second at real wind speed, plus a turbulence weight) from the albedo: the measured zonal jet profile (Porco et al. 2003, Limaye 1986, read to about 15 m/s) and hand-placed vortices (the Red Spot at 21.5 S in this map, the ovals) make a guide field; the surface shader displaces the albedo backwards along the flow in two phases half a cycle apart, each resetting before its displacement smears, a triangle weight handing over between them and a per-region phase offset keeping the resets from lining up. A first bake used the guide field directly and failed: displacements of a dozen texels across cloud features made the two phases blend a doubled, smeared Spot, and the bands turned to mush. The direction now comes from the picture itself, a structure tensor's contour direction (the orientation along which the image changes least) oriented by the guide and driven at its speed, so every feature slides along its own contour and the shear on either side of a vortex turns its rings the right way; the guide is used as is only where the picture has no contour, and cross-contour curl noise is kept small (8 m/s) and confined by the shear weight. The slot is the 41st texture (the fork's descriptor count follows), the map is optional at load (a neutral map and a warning when absent), and a Gas giant panel section holds the switch back to the warp, the wind speed (1500x real), the cycle (12 s) and the turbulence. Surface pass in the Jupiter view: 0.99 ms against 0.95.
- Close-up detail on the gas giant is baked, flow-aligned filaments rather than isotropic noise: tools/bake-flow-map.py also writes gas_detail.png (4096x2048, grayscale), three octaves of noise integrated along the wind map's streamlines (line integral convolution, each octave at the resolution its scale needs) and normalised to standard deviations. The shader multiplies it into the albedo at magnification, on the advected coordinate, at 0.11 per standard deviation, and keeps only the finest isotropic octave beyond the 4K the map holds. Real cloud detail is filaments drawn out along the wind, and around the vortices the streamlines are their rings, so the Spot gains a spiral texture that the painted map lacks. The map is the 42nd texture slot, optional like the flow map (flat when absent). The read replaces a three-octave noise call, so the surface pass in the Jupiter view at 1080p gets cheaper: 0.90 ms against 0.99 with the flow map alone and 0.95 before either.
- The gas giant's albedo is Cassini's December 2000 cylindrical map (NASA/JPL/Space Science Institute, Photojournal PIA07782, public domain), 3600x1800 after cropping the TIFF's repeated wrap column and pole row. A survey of free global Jupiter maps (Cassini PIA07782; Hubble OPAL 2015 to 2025 from STScI, NASA SVS and ESA/Hubble; JunoCam perijove maps; Commons derivatives) found none with more real detail than 3600 wide, and found that the Solar System Scope map used until now is a softened, recoloured copy of the Cassini map at 2048. So the switch keeps the picture, the vortex placements and the look, and gains 1.75x real resolution with full latitude coverage and a clean wrap. The OPAL maps show the 2024 planet but in narrow-band false colour with the poles missing; the ESA 7200 px map is an upscale with a seam and a cut at 80 degrees; Björn Jónsson's maps are non-commercial. The import script gained a crop option for sources that repeat their wrap column, the bake tool's vortex list was re-read from the new map, and the magnification constant follows the new texel count. The bake tool's bilinear read also wraps its integer index, since float32 modulo can land exactly on the width.
- The gas giant is lit as a scattering body rather than a Lambert surface, and its cloud tops have relief. Lighting: the deck stays Lambert (Jupiter's continuum Minnaert exponent is measured at 0.9 to 1.0, so limb darkening is not the deck's doing) but wraps a few degrees past the terminator (panel: terminator softness, 0.08), and a thin haze lies over it whose view path grows as 1/cos toward the limb (panel: limb haze, optical depth 0.08). The haze is forward scattering (Henyey-Greenstein, g 0.5), so at full phase it veils the deck with little light of its own and the limb darkens into a bluish grey, at crescent phases it lights up into a rim, and being high it stays sunlit past the terminator and carries the glow across; the atmosphere shell is unchanged. Relief: no height map of Jupiter's clouds exists, so tools/bake-flow-map.py models one from what is known (zones are high ammonia cloud and belts clearings into deeper cloud, tops varying over some 30 km after Galileo's PIA00489; the Great Red Spot 8 km above its surroundings, the ovals assumed at half): band-scale brightness as height, storms and swirls at a smaller amplitude, the vortex list as flat-topped domes, the filament detail as the finest ridges. gas_relief.png (2048x1024) holds the east and south slopes with the height in alpha, and the shader advects it with the albedo and tilts the shading normal by the panel's exaggeration (6x). Three things had to change on the way. The exaggerated tilt embossed the whole disc under a high sun, so it fades to 15 percent where the sun is more than 30 degrees up, where the true slopes are invisible anyway. And the map was first a normal map: at grazing light its 8-bit quantisation drew the texel rows as stripes across the flat zones, because the slopes that matter are a few thousandths and sit between two levels; storing the slopes square-root encoded (full scale 0.1, the physical peak is 0.05) gives them ten times the levels and the stripes are gone. The first cut also traced the height along the terminator for cast shadows, the Moon's trace with a coarser mip; on a height field this smooth its six discrete steps drew coherent contour bands, so it went, and the height stays in the map only for the record. And the wrapped lighting exposed the planet-anchored shadow map: light now reaches a few degrees past the geometric terminator, where a depth map self-shadows a sphere in texel-stepped bands, worst seen from a low angle over the limb. The giant no longer reads the map; its shadows were already all analytic (the bodies' penumbrae and the belt's). Under a high sun nothing changes; along the terminator the band edges and the ovals gain the shaded sides the Juno images show. Surface pass in the Jupiter view at 1080p, same session: 1.03 ms against 1.01.
- Lightning on the gas giant's night side. Galileo imaged 26 night-side storms up to 1500 km across, the flashes themselves bell-shaped spots of 45 to 80 km half-width, concentrated near 50 degrees in both hemispheres (the north more) in regions of cyclonic shear; Juno measured peak rates of four flashes a second and none near the equator. The surface shader picks storm cells by hash on a 512x256 grid over the map, weighted by that latitude profile and by the flow map's shear weight, each set living about two and a half minutes with a soft handover; every storm flashes at random moments (a hash per eighth-second slot), each flash a 12 ms rise and a 120 ms decay, some striking twice, a hot core of 60 km sigma with a wider glow of cloud lit from below, tinted by the albedo. Where the core is smaller than a pixel it widens to the pixel and dims by the area ratio, so a flash keeps its energy and stays a spark for the bloom from any distance instead of vanishing between texels. It shows only where the sun does not, fading in over the last few degrees of twilight. The panel sets the rate over the planet (4 a second, Juno's peak) and the brightness. The 3x3 cell walk runs only on the night side; the surface pass in the Jupiter view is unchanged within noise.
- Polar cyclone clusters replace the map's smeared poles. The Cassini map is oblique above about 75 degrees and its equirectangular pinch showed from any polar view. Juno found each pole holds a central cyclone ringed near 84 degrees by circumpolar cyclones, eight of 4,000 to 4,600 km in the north and five of 5,600 to 7,000 km in the south, with winds of 55 to 95 m/s (Adriani et al. 2018, Nature 555). tools/bake-flow-map.py now also bakes gas_polar.png and gas_polar_flow.png, an atlas of two azimuthal projections (north above south) of the cap within 20 degrees of each pole, so there is no pinch to hide: a flow of Rankine vortices at those sizes and positions turning with the planet (cyclonic at both poles), a slow circumpolar drift with large folded eddies between them, and an albedo that is the map itself beyond 15 degrees from the pole and, nearer, a planar projection of a clean patch of the map's own 67 to 77 degree cloud texture (the triplanar trick for one pole, suggested in review), crossfaded in between 11 and 15 degrees, with filaments integrated along the cyclones' flow confined to them, a darker eye and brighter ring per cyclone, a few white pop-up ovals and a hint of the blue-grey JunoCam shows toward the pole. Three cuts led there. A flat mean of the map's colour at 70 to 74 degrees, shifted almost half way to blue-grey, under crisp filaments with a five degree blend read as planted. The map's own colour sampled at each cap point, with quieter filaments and a ten degree blend, joined seamlessly but looked simplified against the map's clouds. Sampling the map's pixels directly brought the clouds but also the map's polar smear, as spokes around the cluster; the planar patch supplies real cloud texture without the smear, and outside it the cap and the map are the same pixels, so there is no margin to see. The shader scales the cap on the sphere by the panel's cap size (1.5 by default, so the clusters draw at one and a half times their measured size, a choice for visual weight), crossfades it into the map over the outer part of its radius (cap blend, half by default) at the panel's cap opacity, advects it on the cap flow with the same two-phase scheme, fades the relief out there, and keeps the map's own detail below. A panel switch turns the caps off, and a missing atlas turns them off at load. One trap on the way: the build copies assets/materials into the build directory, so a map dropped in after a build is not seen until the next one and the loader's fallback silently keeps the old poles. The cap costs three texture reads in the polar band only; the surface pass in the Jupiter view is unchanged within noise.
- The gas giant's cloud deck is two decks in the shader. The zones' high ammonia clouds ride above the belts' deeper cloud, so at an oblique view the zones should slide over the belts, and at a low sun shade them along their edges. The upper deck is the albedo sampled displaced toward the viewer by a lift that grows with the view's obliquity (panel: deck lift, 0.0015 texture units at 45 degrees, about 650 km, many times the physical height since the true 20 km would be invisible), masked by the baked relief height (smoothstep over its middle, so the mask is the zone-belt division), over the undisplaced albedo as the lower deck; and the lower deck is darkened where the upper deck lies one tap up-sun of it (panel: deck shadow, 0.3). Both are single bilinear taps on smooth fields, so unlike the removed terminator trace there is no stepping to band. Both advection phases get the treatment so the decks stay continuous through the handover. Under a straight-on view nothing changes; obliquely the effect is a few grey levels concentrated along zone edges and around the vortices, which reads as motion parallax when the camera moves rather than as a feature in a still. Six extra texture reads per giant pixel: surface pass at 1080p, same session, 1.20 ms against 1.05 in the Jupiter view, 2.68 against 2.50 in the belt view. A distance fade (the lift measured in screen pixels, the decks fading out below about one) was tried and dropped: it removed the cost only with the giant small on screen (the Earth view, 1.45 against 1.42), and in every view where the giant fills the screen the lift is still several pixels, so it saved nothing where the cost is paid. Since the effect reads as motion parallax rather than as anything in a still, the panel switch for it was first off by default; on review in motion it is on.
- The surface shader is split by body kind. One 680-line fragment shader had served the Earth, the gas giant, the Moon and Mars and the rocks through a per-instance kind branch. The branch is uniform per draw, so nothing diverged, but a GPU allocates registers for the heaviest path in a shader, and the giant's path (two-phase advection, relief, haze, lightning, the polar caps, the two decks) had become that path for every rock pixel too. Measured before the split by leaving the giant's code compiled but behind a condition never true at run time: carrying it cost the belt view 0.19 ms and the Jupiter view 0.07 ms of surface pass, on top of the giant's own pixel work (0.61 and 0.19 ms). Now surface.slang holds only the vertex stage every surface pipeline shares, surface_common.slang the varyings, the mode and kind numbers (defined once in scene_shared.h and used by the C++ enums), the tangent frame, the material, the sun visibility every body starts from and the solid bodies' BRDF, and each kind has its own fragment shader and pipeline: surface_earth (with the cloud shell), surface_giant, surface_airless (Moon and Mars), surface_rock (with the billboards and the splat mask, and an alpha-blended billboard pipeline, since the billboards had been drawn on the cloud pipeline for its blend). The bodies bind their kind's pipeline per draw; the rock multi-draw keeps one. In the same pass the helpers that had been written three times went into common.slang: the Henyey-Greenstein and Cornette-Shanks phase functions (the belt dust, the giant's haze and the atmospheres each had a copy) and the two luma weightings (six copies), and the splat area constant moved from the culling and disc-splat shaders into the rock class include both use. Purity was checked by capturing all six bookmarks before and after: the differences (mean under 0.4 of a grey level) are below the run-to-run noise of the same build captured twice (mean 0.7 in the belt view, from tumbling rocks and the temporal pass). Surface pass at 1080p, same session: belt view 2.34 ms against 2.45, Jupiter view 1.06 against 1.08; less than the dormant-code experiment promised, so the rock shader carries most of its own register pressure, and the split's larger value is that each material now compiles, iterates and profiles alone.
- Exposure adaptation advances every frame. The meter reads back every 16 frames, and the adaptation filter had only been evaluated when a readback landed, with the whole interval's elapsed time applied at once: at 60 Hz and the 0.6 s darkening constant that moved the exposure a third of the way to its target in one jump every 16 frames, a staircase that showed when the scene's brightness changed. The readback now only sets the target; the first-order filter runs toward it every frame with that frame's elapsed time, so the exposure glides and a new target changes only its slope. The rates, the asymmetry and the clamp are unchanged.

- The gas giant's close-range detail hands over from the baked filament map to per-pixel streaks. Within a few thousand kilometres of the deck the 4K filament map is magnified past its texels and bilinear filtering draws them as a comb of streaks, which read as mud. Once a filament texel covers more than about four pixels the map's weight fades to a third, and streaks made per pixel take over: four taps of one noise octave averaged along the local wind direction from the flow map, the bake's line integral convolution done at whatever scale the view needs, so the detail stays wind-aligned and has no texel structure at any distance; a finer octave arrives closer in. The first cut evaluated the streaks inside the per-phase, per-deck detail call, four times a pixel, and cost 3 ms in a full-screen close-up; evaluated once per pixel on the phase-blended advected coordinate (continuous through the handover, since a phase resets only while its weight is zero) with four taps, it costs 0.15 ms there (surface pass 1.81 ms against 1.66) and nothing at ordinary distances, where the branch is skipped. Aside from the measuring: the --time option puts the camera into tour mode at that time, which overrides --bookmark, so stills across an animation cycle are taken by frame count instead.
- Motion streaks give the camera's movement a cue. A lattice of dust motes is fixed in space around the camera (4x4x4 cells of 5 units, 16 hashed motes each, 1024 in all), so they stream past with real parallax; each is drawn as a soft streak from where it was on screen last frame to where it is now, using the previous frame's projection and camera delta the temporal pass already carries. The streak is the mote's own screen motion, so it points along the movement and grows with speed, and a still camera shows nothing (it fades in between 2 and 12 pixels of motion); long streaks spread their light so a fast pass reads as lines rather than bars, and motes fade at the lattice's edge and within a third of a unit of the lens. One instanced draw of 1024 quads into the HDR image after the dust composite, depth tested against the scene so bodies occlude them, additive; drawn before the temporal pass, which reprojects them correctly because they are world-fixed points at their own depth, so they do not ghost. On review the streaks showed on a pure turn too, since a turn moves everything on screen; the previous position is now projected through this frame's view rather than last frame's, so the streak is the camera's translation alone and a turn in place shows nothing. Panel: a Motion streaks switch and an intensity in the Post FX section. Cost while panning at 1080p, same session: 3.50 ms against 3.34 for the whole frame, mostly the extra render pass and its barriers.
- The sky's stars are the Yale Bright Star Catalogue. The procedural field had drawn every star at one brightness; real counts grow with faintness, about 2.5 times per magnitude in the textbook figure and 3.1 times per magnitude in the catalogue's complete bins (magnitudes 0 to 5, from 11 to 3,419 stars), so the render was flat where the sky is steep. tools/bake-stars.py bakes the catalogue (public domain, 9,096 usable of 9,110 stars to magnitude 6.5) into 32-byte records, 284 KB, that the star pass reads as vertices: one instanced Gaussian point per star, about a pixel wide, whose integrated energy is the star's linear flux, coloured from its B-V index, drawn additively over the background before the bodies paint over it. The distribution is then the catalogue's by construction; on the 8-bit captures the check is coarse (the faintest bin is film grain and tonemapping compresses the bright end), but the three usable bins fall 74, 16, 3, a steep law. The procedural field stays for the stars the catalogue stops at, magnitudes 6.5 to 9 on the same law (most at the faint end) and on the same brightness scale, and the fake galactic band now lies on the real galactic plane in the catalogue's sky frame, so the bright stars of the Milky Way sit in it. A panel gain (2 by default) lifts the stars against the planets, since the display cannot hold the eye's range; a Sky section holds it with the catalogue switch, and a missing file keeps the procedural sky. Two facts for the record: the catalogue's brightest is Sirius at -1.46, and the Sky frame is equatorial J2000 with no relation to the scene's bodies, which are fiction. One instanced draw of 9,096 quads: surface pass in the Jupiter view at 1080p, same session, 1.35 ms against 1.28. The baked colours are the blackbody's at full chroma, which read as overblown on review (hot stars strongly blue, cool ones orange, where the eye sees faint tints); the shader mixes them toward white by a panel star colour, half by default, and the bake stays physical.
- The Milky Way is ESO's all-sky panorama at 1024x512, reconstructed by a cubic B-spline. The band had been a noise-shaped glow; the panorama (ESO/S. Brunier, CC BY 4.0) is galactic-centred, so tools/bake-milkyway.py decodes it to linear light, low-passes it to the target texel and decimates, gamma encodes, and runs the cubic B-spline prefilter (Unser: a causal and an anticausal recursive pass per axis with pole sqrt(3) - 2, periodic in longitude, mirrored at the poles) so the stored texels are spline coefficients; the background shader converts the view ray to galactic coordinates (the IAU pole at J2000, in the same equatorial frame the star catalogue uses, checked on the north celestial pole and the Large Magellanic Cloud to a hundredth of a degree) and reconstructs with the four-tap B-spline, which then interpolates the decimated panorama exactly rather than blurring it, and decodes after reconstruction. The worry was a texel lattice from so small a map at six pixels per texel; the test was the same view captured with plain bilinear on the unfiltered map and with the spline on the coefficients, magnified four times: neither shows a lattice, the low-pass having removed anything sharper than a texel, and the spline is marginally smoother in the dark gradients at no cost worth counting. Review found the 1024 map blotchy all the same, and rightly: the heavy low-pass (half the decimation factor) left the band genuinely soft, and the noise that had shaped the fake band, fourteen cycles over the sphere, was still painting twenty-degree blotches at twenty percent onto the photograph. The map is now 2048x1024 at 4.4 MB with a lighter low-pass, and the modulation is fine grain at a fraction of a degree and a quarter of the amplitude, standing for the unresolved star clouds. Review also found the colours far from the photograph: the map itself matched the source to a percent, and the belt in front was not it either; every tone curve inflated the chroma, PBR Neutral most (red over green 1.84 against the source's 1.20), because after exposure for a planet the band sits at a few hundredths in the curves' toes, which crush the darkest channel, and the panorama is display-referred already, so it was being tone mapped twice. For the default curve the toe is a black offset with a closed-form inverse, and lifting the exposed sky by it in the background brought the ratio to 1.37, but it also uncrushed everything the toe had hidden, the photograph's own sky glow and the faint procedural stars, while the catalogue stars in their own pass stayed crushed, so the sky went grey and inconsistent; the lift was dropped. The fix is in the display transform instead: the neutral curve's black offset is a flare subtraction meant for well-exposed product shots, and on a scene that is mostly dark sky it crushes dim neutrals threefold, so it is now scaled by a panel black offset (Post FX; 1 is the published curve, 0 none), 0.3 by default, which brings the bulge to 1.45 over green with the dark sky at 6 of 255 rather than 2; the photograph's own sky glow (linear 0.0015) is subtracted in the bake so the scene's black stays black; and the lens glare was checked and cleared as a contributor. ACES and AgX have milder toes and are untouched. A last review caught the photograph's own point stars: decimated to specks, they had survived in the map and doubled the catalogue's stars as speckle. The bake now removes them with a median over a seven-texel disc at the target scale (1.2 degrees), which drops features smaller than half the disc and keeps the edges of everything larger, dust lanes included, followed by a light blur; a grey-scale opening was tried first and rejected, since its square element left the band as a mosaic of plateaus. The map holds only the diffuse light, and the catalogue draws the stars. Even star-free and sharper (a five-texel median, a lighter low-pass), the smooth map read as blotches rather than a galaxy, and rightly: the band's character is granular, millions of unresolved stars, which no map at this size can hold. So the band's light is now drawn as grains in the background shader: a lattice forty times denser than the star field's (1,400 cells per radian), a grain in a cell where a hash falls under the map's brightness there, each a Gaussian normalised to unit integral so the grains' sum averages to the band's radiance at any resolution (the crop's mean brightness matched the smooth map's within a fifth of a level); the kernel widens with the pixel footprint and hands back to the smooth map where it grows past a cell, so a distant band is milky and a close one resolves into star clouds. Two false starts before it: squared value noise as grain drew its cubic lattice as a mosaic, and its first frequencies were written as if in texels rather than radians, so both octaves fell below a pixel and vanished. Twenty-seven hashes per background pixel: surface pass in the Jupiter view at 1080p, same session, 1.52 ms against 1.35 with the band off, the frame 6.05 against 5.79. The Milky Way is diffuse, so a megabyte holds it and the catalogue stars supply everything sharp; a panel brightness and switch sit in the Sky section, and a missing map keeps the procedural band. Surface pass in the Jupiter view at 1080p, same session: 1.41 ms against 1.37 with the band off.
- The Milky Way is a procedural galaxy model, not a photograph. The panorama route (ESO's all-sky image as spline coefficients, star-free, sharpened, drawn as grains) never lost its blotchiness on review: a map of that size holds the band's large shapes but none of its character, and every treatment of it was a treatment of blur. tools/bake-galaxy.py instead builds the galaxy in galactic coordinates from the literature (geometry after Bland-Hawthorn and Gerhard 2016): thin and thick exponential stellar discs (scale lengths 3.2 and 3.6 kpc, heights 0.32 and 0.9), a triaxial bar-bulge with its long axis 28 degrees from the Sun-centre line, four logarithmic spiral arms of 12.5 degree pitch carrying a bluer young population, and a dust disc thinner than the stars (0.13 kpc) clumped by three octaves of noise with gaps between the clouds, reddening more in blue than red, and absent from the Local Bubble, the dust-free cavity some 150 pc across that the Sun sits in. Each map texel is a direction from the Sun, 8.2 kpc out and 20 pc above the plane; the emission is integrated outward through the dust's transmittance in 96 steps spaced quadratically so the nearby dust that makes the rift is resolved. The dynamic range is compressed above the band's own level only, so the faint sky stays dark and the centre folds to a few times the band. The map keeps the radiance in RGB and the dust optical depth in alpha, both as spline coefficients; at render time the shader redistributes the stored dust by noise into filaments below the texel and the band's light into star-cloud grains, the transparency and the density extrapolated from the model rather than invented. Three rounds of the model at 512 wide fixed what the first showed: normalising to the bulge crushed the local band (real skies show the centre only a few times the band), a dust layer with no gaps gave a hairline rift, and clouds beside the Sun covered the whole sky in shadow until the Local Bubble was carved out. Nothing is downloaded; the model's parameters are the tunables and the seed changes the clouds.
- The Milky Way is a cloud of Gaussian splats fitted to ESO's panorama, summed per pixel. The galaxy model rendered as a grey wash: its halo sat a tenth of the band's level over the whole sky where the photograph has a hundredth, and the model was a fiction anyway. On review the proposal was to compress a star-free reference into a formula or a cloud of splats, and that is what tools/bake-splats.py does: the panorama (eso0932a, CC BY 4.0) is median filtered at two scales so its stars go and its lanes stay, its blacked-out patches (the photographer's horizon) are masked out of the fit and of the sky floor, which is the third percentile of the valid sky, and a cloud of anisotropic Gaussians on the sphere is fitted to it by gradient descent in PyTorch (Adam, 1,500 to 3,000 steps on batches of directions at 512x256). Each splat is a centre, two widths in angle along a rotated tangent frame (azimuthal equidistant coordinates, so the shape is right anywhere on the sphere, with no antipode ghost), and a signed RGB amplitude, the negative ones carving the dust lanes out of the positive ones (a third of them end up negative); widths are held between 1.2 and 45 degrees, so nothing star-sized can be fitted, and the aspect within four, since the first fits drew long brush strokes. The first loss was the area-weighted squared error in a square-root space, so the faint outer band counts as much as the bulge; even with the dark sky weighted fivefold it kept forgiving a haze of a hundredth of the bulge over the whole sky, the tails of the broad halo splats, so the loss is now the squared log ratio over a floor of a hundredth, which prices that haze the same as a wrong bulge. The first fits ran on the LAN machine's cores (twelve minutes for 256 splats, twenty-five for 512, about a second a step per 512); on review the ask was for a thousand or more, fitted here on the GPU, where the same script runs a step of 256 in 40 ms, so 3,000 steps of 1,024 take minutes. The renderer reads the records (64 bytes a splat) as vertices and sums them in shaders/galaxy.slang; at the frame's resolution that cost 3.7 ms for 256 splats in a sky-filling view at 1080p, so the sum is drawn at a quarter of the frame into an rgba16 target (the same size as the bloom's) and the background samples it bilinearly, which is exact enough since nothing in it is sharper than 1.2 degrees, twenty pixels at this field of view: 0.39 ms for 256 splats and 0.98 for 512, same session, on a frame of 2.16. That cost still grew linearly with the count (1.9 microseconds a splat), so rather than a culling pass at run time the bake buckets the splats by the cells of a 7.5 degree longitude/latitude grid their reach cones meet, a table after the records (65 splats a cell on average for 512, against the 512 the loop visited), and the pass looks up its pixel's cell and sums that list, pointed at the table through the root's second pointer: the render is unchanged to the frame's noise floor and the cost follows the splats in view rather than the cloud, in the same sky-filling view 0.47 ms for 512 (against 0.98 without the table), 0.72 for 1,024 and 1.30 for 2,048. The default is 1,024 (266 KB with the table): at this field of view 2,048 adds little the eye finds, since the softness is the 1.2 degree floor on the widths rather than the count, and that floor is the knob to turn if a sharper band is wanted, at the quarter-resolution pass's limit of about 0.6 degrees. On review the floor went to 0.6 degrees (a bake option, the target 1,024 wide so three pixels resolve it): the 1,024-splat fit then shows lanes the 1.2 degree fit blurred, at 1.46 ms against 1.43 in the sky-filling view (the reach cones shrink with the widths, so the finer fit is no dearer), and 2,048 splats at 0.6 degrees add a little more structure for 0.54 ms more, so 1,024 at 0.6 stays the default and the count is the panel's to lower or a refit's to raise. The dust fBm's three octaves of gradient noise at every background pixel cost 0.4 ms in that view; the dark sky now skips them and the gradients go unnormalised. Nothing is sampled from a map any more, so there is no texel lattice at any zoom and no star residue, and the map, its slot's texture and the two earlier bakes are gone; the target keeps the slot. The star-cloud grains and the dust filaments stay on top; the filaments take their depth from the dark splats' share of the light, and the first pass of that at the old scale modulated the band fourfold and showed the value noise's lattice, so the depth is a mild two at most and the clumping half what it was. The fit is a low-pass of the photograph at a degree or so, a soft wide-field look that more splats sharpen. Playtesting found the band turning to speckle toward the frame's corners and, in full screen, everywhere: the star-cloud grains switched on by a variance threshold that sat right at 1080p's cell per pixel, so a rectilinear view's corners (less angle per pixel) and any higher resolution crossed it, and 1,400 cells a radian at one or two pixels a cell is dithering, not star clouds. Gating the grains to a cell per four pixels cut the corner's high-frequency residual from 2.7 to 0.8 levels, the film grain's own, and also made them a dead control: the camera has distance but no field-of-view zoom, so that magnification never happens, and on review the grains were removed. The dust filaments were two octaves of value noise, whose cubic lattice read as blotches; a ridged five-octave version read as a vein network; on review the ask was a plain fBm of three tiers with its parameters exposed, so the lanes' depth is now modulated by three octaves of gradient noise (Perlin, a quintic fade, since value noise draws its cube grid) on lattices turned against each other, with the base feature size, amplitude, lacunarity and gain in the Sky panel (set on review to 1.4 degrees, 2, 3 and 0.7), each octave fading out toward two pixels a cell and the mean transmittance kept. Its first wiring converted the size to radians as if the lattice noise took cycles, which ran the base octave at three pixels a cell, so the fade cut everything above a degree and the finer octaves never showed, and gain and lacunarity did nothing; found on review, fixed in the units. The Sky panel also holds the splats drawn (the bake orders the records by energy, amplitude times area, so the first n are the ones that matter, and the pass skips the rest; up to 4,096) and the band's brightness, whose default fell from 1 to 0.1 on review once the Gaia fits, which keep the sky's real halo, went in; at that level the render looked flat against the references, and measurement showed why: the chain is linear throughout, but the fit halves the compact bright core (0.30 against the target's 0.59 at the centre) while holding the band and halo, and 0.1 puts the band near a physical surface brightness, which is faint by nature. So the pass gained a band contrast exponent (Sky panel, default 1) about the fit's unit, for the photographic look, and the bake's loss a square-root brightness weight to hold the core.
- The Milky Way's splat cloud is fitted in stages at the coherent residuals, the recipe from the peer machine. My first allocation (half the splats at the start by the image gradient, the rest every 500 steps at the per-pixel squared log error with the residual's colour) cut the error 14 percent on a smoke test but on the Gaia map chased individual stars and speckle: dots, hairs and, after a low-pass, an aspect cap, brightness weighting and broader seeds, confetti over the halo. The RTX 4080 machine, working from the handoff, found the working form (its reports are under .scratch/milky-way/peer/, CANDIDATES.md first): a coarse cloud of 256 splats by brightness, then growth in stages to 512, 1024, 1536, 2048, 3072 and 4096, each new centre sampled from the signed log residual averaged over 4x4 blocks before squaring, so isolated pixels count for little and lanes for much, each newcomer narrow and of zero amplitude so growing preserves the image, every later stage refining all splats at a fifth of the coarse rates with the latent widths projected back into range after each step. On the cartesian 2k Gaia map it beat the plain fit at equal count on every measure the peer took from the exported records (band error down 9.9 percent at 2048 splats for 37 percent fewer bytes and 57 percent fewer cell-list entries per pixel; 19 percent down at 4096 splats and a 0.3 degree floor for the plain 2048's bytes), while its further experiments (leave-one-out recycling, a multiscale gradient term, a cell-work penalty, GaussianImage++ style filter release, spatially spread growth) each moved the result by under a percent and none was promoted. The recipe is ported into bake-splats.py as the progressive path (stage ladder, growth, refinement rates, projection, .pt checkpoints with --resume, --compact 32x16 tables), the peer's validation and analysis helpers into tools/, and the peer's 4096-splat 0.3 degree fit with 1,500 further refinement updates (its v5 control, in the compact 32x16 table variant, 391 KB) is the shipped asset, from the 1800x900 cartesian JPEG (ESA/Gaia/DPAC, CC BY-SA 3.0 IGO, a share-alike licence, which the manifest records). Its halo is the map's logarithmic stretch, not a photograph's range, which is why the panel's brightness default is 0.1 and a contrast exponent exists; a fit on an unstretched target is the open experiment, with the floor and count. The peer's costs are work proxies; the pass's frame cost with this asset is still to be measured here, and at a 0.3 degree floor the quarter-resolution pass softens the finest splats (1.4 samples per sigma), the half setting resolves them.
- The Milky Way's seam is the source's border. Playtesting showed a vertical seam where the Gaia map's edges meet: the cartesian JPEG's outermost columns and rows are a lighter border (mean 35 against 23 beside them at the sides, 17 against 4 at the top and bottom), and the fit reproduced that line with narrow splats, 138 within 3 degrees of the seam where a uniform share is 68; the render itself wraps correctly (the fit's edge columns differ less than adjacent ones). The map preparation now replaces the border columns and rows with their neighbours before anything else, and the asset is refitted.
